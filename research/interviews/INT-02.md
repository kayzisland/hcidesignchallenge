## Full notes by topic

### The last time he studied
- His last study session was for an exam, to fill a gap in his knowledge of a specific field, covering its timeline and topics. He did it entirely by his own choice.
- He noticed the gap through social media and a forum-style website, where people were discussing a subject he felt he should know. It made him feel he had to catch up and was missing out, a kind of social pressure.

### Feeling behind
- He felt he was missing out and inadequate. He had always been on top of things during his degree, and was suddenly in a position where he was falling behind.
- He realised how little he knew.

### Too many resources
- The sheer number of resources left him unable to choose: picking option 37 out of 200 and wondering what he was missing.
- He worried whether his source was correct and accurate compared with others, or more detailed, or more practical.
- There was no clear path to the relevant information.
- What makes a resource good for him is being practical: how easily he can apply it to other problems.

### Main challenges
- **Long-term consistency.** Starting something and keeping it up for a month or two is no problem. Once he sees progress, he stops pushing.
- **Distraction.** He juggles several learning paths at once.
- **A changing field.** The field keeps changing, with conflicting views on where it is heading and a risk that what he learns becomes obsolete.

### Distractions
- Without outside pressure, he ends up on his phone.
- He always thinks he should have started earlier.

### Using LLMs
- He doesn't go to LLMs first. He uses human and forum-based sources.
- He calls himself old-fashioned and prefers older ways of learning.
- He might use an LLM in an emergency, if nothing else works.

### When something is very difficult
- He goes down rabbit holes.
- If he can't solve it, he can become obsessed with it, to his own detriment.
- Ultimately he asks someone else if he can't solve it.
- He never gives up on a topic, but he may come back to it later.

### How long hard topics take
- The longest he has spent on one section was an hour on 2-3 minutes of a recording before moving on.
- A topic that takes an hour is usually very niche. In his experience, most topics explained well will generally only take a few minutes, and he does look elsewhere for explanations.
- During his dissertation, some small topics took well over a week.

### How he learns best
- Visual cues help him a lot, especially combined with audio, even random squares and arrows on a page. He likes spoken explanations with visual cues.
- Complex maths with lots of symbols is hard to digest; jargon makes his brain switch off.
- When material looks strange or alien, it puts him off academically.

### His study workflow
- He starts with a past paper to identify the topics he knows and doesn't know, which gives him immediate feedback.
- He then goes through all the lectures anyway, and makes notes even on topics he already knows.
- He uses the transcript to spot areas he knows and makes fewer notes on them. The transcript lets him condense the next two minutes to check whether the lecturer is still on topic.
- An inaccurate transcript frustrates him.

### Views on study apps, flashcards and quizzes
- He sees flashcards and quizzes as a gimmick because of the time it takes to make them, but he would use them if they were provided.
- Most of what he knows about study apps comes from promotional content about their features, which puts him off a lot. He believes that if they really worked, he would hear more about how they had helped people.
- He would want real case studies and demos of someone actually working with the app and benefiting, not polished demos.

### What would help him get started
- Seeing the tool used in a real, practical scenario, with an explanation of how it helped.
- In a lecture, knowing where the topic fits in the real world. That also lets him pick what to actually study.
- He is more interested in practical application than theory.
